## 1. Point-by-point against the concept

| Concept element | Sourcegraph equivalent | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Proposition/assertion split** | **Nothing.** SCIP has `Relationship` (4 booleans) and `Occurrence`. A fact exists or does not; there is no row representing "someone said it." | No equivalent. Not a design difference — a categorical absence. |
| **Content-addressed edge fingerprint** | **Partial, different purpose.** SCIP symbol strings are a real grammar (`scheme manager package version (descriptor)+`) and function as stable content-addressed *node* ids across repos and package versions. There is no fingerprint for an *edge*. | Their node addressing is better than yours and worth stealing (§4). Edge addressing: nothing. |
| **Polarity / refutation** | **Nothing.** The SCIP spec explicitly says indexers sit "somewhere on the spectrum between precision and heuristics" and that "complementary information can be merged together from multiple sources" — merge with **no arbitration mechanism**. Two indexers that disagree produce two facts and the consumer cannot tell. | No equivalent. Their model cannot express disagreement at all. |
| **Corroboration as a count** | **Nothing.** Merging is set-union, not counting. | No equivalent. |
| **Bi-temporal validity** | **Nothing at the fact level.** Temporality is entirely git-derived at query time: `rev:at.time(...)`, `type:diff`, `type:commit`, `repo:has.commit.after(...)`, Code Insights backfill. | Their approach is *strictly stronger for code* and *strictly absent for everything else*. Git is a perfect bitemporal store of file contents, and they exploit it well. It says nothing about when a *belief* was held. Your approach is better for the problem you have; theirs is better for the problem they have, and costs them nothing to maintain. |
| **Scope sweeps (closed-world snapshot)** | **Partial, at a coarser grain.** Re-indexing a repo *replaces* that repo's index wholesale — which is a scope sweep with `scope_key = (repo, indexer)` and no explicit expiry row. Data retention policies expire whole uploads on schedule. | Their mechanism is simpler and more reliable than yours *within its scope*, because replacement is atomic and the closed world is enforced by the indexer, not by tagging. It fails the moment a fact spans two scopes — which is every cross-repo edge you care about. Yours is the right design; steal the atomicity. |
| **Predicate cardinality (functional predicates)** | **Nothing.** Four boolean relations, no cardinality semantics. | No equivalent. |
| **Trust score** | **Nothing quantitative.** There is a two-tier *qualitative* trust model — "precise" (compiler-derived) vs "search-based" (heuristic) — and "Sourcegraph automatically uses Precise Code Navigation whenever available, and Search-based Code Navigation is used as a fallback." Critically, **the consumer is not told which tier answered**, nor how old the index was. | Their model is a one-bit trust score, silently applied. Yours is richer and exposed. But note the adversarial reading: Sourcegraph, with unlimited engineering budget and eight years in this problem, concluded that a *binary* provenance tier plus "prefer the compiler" was sufficient. Your `trust = base × freshness × anchor_integrity × corroboration` has four free parameters and no labelled data. Their one bit is at least calibrated. |
| **anchor_integrity (evidence path unchanged at HEAD)** | **Partial and it is the strongest thing they have here.** Precise navigation is keyed to a commit; when the index is stale relative to HEAD, Sourcegraph falls back to search. Your own risk register is right: anchor_integrity cannot distinguish a reformat from a rewrite. Sourcegraph's answer — *degrade the mechanism, not the score* — is arguably more honest. Consider making low anchor_integrity trigger re-verification rather than a number. |
| **Entity resolution: strong ids** | **Partial and excellent within code.** SCIP `Package{manager, name, version}` plus `Index.external_symbols` is a genuine strong-identifier system with a uniqueness contract, and it resolves across repo *and* package-dependency boundaries. It has zero coverage of a Redis instance, an ARN, a Terraform address or an OTel service name. Your `entity_identifier (authority, value)` primary key is the right generalisation of what they did. |
| **Entity resolution: weak aliases, merge/distinct, read-time canonicalization** | **Nothing.** No alias table, no merge log, no negative-evidence table. Symbol equality is string equality. | No equivalent. Note the flip side: because they never guess, they never over-merge. Your `entity_merge`/`entity_distinct` tables exist to clean up a mess Sourcegraph structurally cannot make. |
| **`env` as a first-class discriminator** | **Nothing.** No environment concept anywhere in the documented model. Repo KVPs could hold `env=prod` as a string, with no semantics. | No equivalent. This is one of your sharper edges. |
| **Call-site grain, derive service edges** | **Partial, and this is the single biggest thing they already have.** `Occurrence` carries `typed_range`, `symbol_roles` (Definition 0x1, Import 0x2, WriteAccess 0x4, ReadAccess 0x8, Generated 0x10, Test 0x20, ForwardDefinition 0x40) and `typed_enclosing_range`, which the schema says is explicitly designed for call hierarchies. Precise cross-repo call-site data at compiler accuracy, fleet-wide, is *exactly* your finest observable grain — for code. | Theirs is better than anything you will derive from LLM inference, by a wide margin, and it is available over MCP today. ## 2. The motivating query, walked concretely

**Question:** "Where does the list of notifications in Client A come from?"
**Required path:** Client A (repo 1) → calls → Service C (repo 3) → reads_from → Redis cache E → falls_back_to → Postgres in AWS → provisioned_by → Terraform (repo 4). Then: "How do I connect to that database?"

**What Sourcegraph would actually do.** You would ask Deep Search, `@`-mentioning the four repos. Its agentic loop would:

1. `keyword_search`/`nls_search` for `notifications` in Client A — plausibly finds `fetchNotifications()` and the URL it hits.
2. `go_to_definition` / `find_references` — **hop 1 succeeds precisely** if the client calls Service C through a generated or shared typed client. If it's a bare `fetch('/api/notifications')`, precise navigation dies at the HTTP boundary and Deep Search falls back to grepping the string `/api/notifications` across the fleet. Usually that works. It is a text match, not a fact.
3. In Service C, search for the handler; find `redis.get('notif:' + id)` and a `catch`/`if (!cached)` branch hitting Postgres. **Hop 2 and hop 3 are LLM reading code.** No `reads_from` edge exists; the model infers it from syntax. Quality is genuinely decent and the source list cites the exact lines.
4. **Hop 4 — the wall.** Which *Redis instance*? The code says `process.env.REDIS_URL`. Deep Search can grep repo 4's Terraform for `aws_elasticache_cluster`, and if the resource name happens to contain `notif`, an LLM will link them. That link is a **string-similarity guess over two text files**, with no identity model behind it, no `env` discrimination, and nothing preventing it from picking the staging cluster. This is precisely the over-merge failure your `entity_distinct` table exists to prevent — except Sourcegraph makes the guess invisibly inside a markdown answer.
5. **"How do I connect to that database?"** — Sourcegraph's universe ends at the repository. It can find a Terraform output block or a `README` that mentions a bastion host. It cannot know the endpoint AWS actually assigned, cannot know which Secrets Manager key holds the credential, cannot know the tunnel is only reachable from the VPN. If someone wrote it in a markdown file, Deep Search finds it; if the only record is in AWS, Sourcegraph has no eyes.

**What it would take to make it work properly.** Configure code-host connections for all four repos; enable precise indexing for the client's and service's languages (Terraform has **no SCIP indexer**, so repo 4 is text-only forever); then hand-author the infrastructure links. Your only place to put a non-code fact is **repository key-value metadata** — flat, untyped, string-keyed, **repo-scoped**. The killer: a KVP attaches to *a repository*, so `reads_from=redis-notif-prod` can only be said about repo 3 as a whole, never about Service C's notification handler, and `redis-notif-prod` is a string that resolves to nothing. You cannot express an edge between two things. You cannot express `falls_back_to` at all — it's a claim *about* an edge, which needs your proposition-as-subject construction, and KVPs have no subject but a repo.

**Verdict:** Sourcegraph answers hops 1–3 well today with zero schema work — better than a cold-start Living AI Knowledge instance, because it needs no ingestion. It answers hop 4 by guessing and hop 5 not at all. And critically, **it re-derives the whole chain, at LLM cost and up to five minutes latency, every single time anyone asks.** Nothing accumulates. The tenth engineer to ask pays the same price as the first, and if the answer was wrong the first time, nobody can record that it was wrong.

## 3. Threat assessment

**Threat level: low-to-medium. Higher on narrative than on product.**

*Incentive:* real and rising. Every 2026 Sourcegraph blog post is about being "the shared intelligence layer for developers and AI agents," and the homepage is already claiming "a continuously updated knowledge base of your codebase." They want this territory.

*What they would have to build:* a general entity/relationship store with provenance (does not exist — SCIP is closed, repo KVPs are flat), an assertion model with polarity and bitemporality, entity resolution across code and infrastructure, and — the big one — **infrastructure ingestion**. Nothing in eight years of their product line points at AWS, Terraform state or OTel. That is a different data-source org, a different sales motion, and it collides head-on with Backstage/Port/Cortex, whom Sourcegraph has never competed with.

*What they already have that you do not:* fleet-wide compiler-accurate cross-repo symbol graphs at call-site grain, indexers for eight language ecosystems, scale to 500k repos, code-host connectors for GitHub/GitLab/Bitbucket/Gerrit/Azure DevOps/Perforce, an agent with write access to code hosts, a live MCP server in thousands of agent configs, and enterprise distribution.

*Countervailing facts:* the platform is **closed source** (`sourcegraph-public-snapshot` archived), they just **lost their founders and their agent** to the Amp spin-out (2025-12-02) and are now a "code search business" under a new CEO, their stated 2026 roadmap is Agentic Batch Changes and outcome-based per-merged-changeset pricing, and their flagship shipping direction is *making changes*, not *accumulating knowledge*.

*The realistic threat path is not a schema.* It is Deep Search getting a cache and a feedback loop: persist conversations (the API already does), let the agent write a durable note, index those notes. That would neutralise the "re-derives every time" critique for maybe 60% of user value without them building a graph at all. Watch for a changelog entry that gives "Living Documentation" a real URL.

*Most likely outcome:* they close the **read** gap for code and never touch infrastructure. You remain uncontested on hops 4–5, which is also the half of your query that is hardest to populate.

## 4. What to steal — concretely

1. **The symbol grammar as a strong identifier for code entities.** `<scheme> <manager> <package-name> <version> (<descriptor>)+` with typed suffixes (Namespace, Type, Term, Method + disambiguator, TypeParameter, Parameter, Meta, Local, Macro). Add `authority='scip_symbol'` to `entity_identifier` and store SCIP symbol strings verbatim. This gives you free, exact, cross-repo, cross-package-version identity for every code entity — precisely the "strong identifier" your resolution ladder wants, and it means a Sourcegraph import can never over-merge two symbols.
2. **`Package{manager, name, version}` as a separate structured column, not a string.** Version-in-identity is why SCIP resolves across dependency boundaries. Your `entity_identifier.value` is a flat string; consider a structured variant for package-scoped entities.
3. **`SymbolRole` as a bitset of qualifiers on an occurrence.** Definition / Import / WriteAccess / ReadAccess / Generated / Test / ForwardDefinition. Three of those map directly onto your evidence records: `Generated` and `Test` are exactly the flags that should *discount* trust (an edge asserted only from generated or test code is weaker), and `ReadAccess`/`WriteAccess` disambiguate `reads_from` vs `writes_to` mechanically. Add a `roles` bitmask or flag set to your `evidence` entries.
4. **`enclosing_range` / `enclosing_symbol`.** Storing the *enclosing* symbol alongside the exact line range makes evidence survive reformatting far better than a line number does. This is a direct, cheap improvement to `anchor_integrity`: if the line moved but the enclosing symbol still exists and still contains matching text, that's a 0.9, not a 0.3. It attacks your own risk #3 head-on.
5. **The precise/search two-tier fallback as a *behaviour*, not a score.** When anchor integrity is low, don't just lower a number — mark the edge `needs_reverification` and enqueue the verification agent. Sourcegraph degrades the mechanism; that is more honest than degrading a float nobody can calibrate.
6. **Retention policies as first-class configuration.** Their model — protected defaults (HEAD forever, tags 1y, branch tips 3mo) plus admin-authored per-repo policies — is a good shape for your `scope_key` sweeps. Make sweep aggressiveness a named, inspectable policy per scope rather than a constant.
7. **Atomic scope replacement.** Their re-index *replaces* an upload atomically. Your sweep tags-then-expires. Consider making a sweep a single transaction with a staging table, so a crashed importer can never half-expire a scope.
8. **API shape: `/api-reference` per instance publishing its own live OpenAPI schema.** Cheap to do in Node, and it solves your predicate-vocabulary discovery problem — an agent can fetch the currently-known predicate list from the running instance rather than hardcoding it.
9. **"Detailed Sources" as a contract on every answer.** Deep Search returns "exactly which searches were performed and which files were read." Make your path-template responses return the full traversal trace — every proposition id, every assertion, every trust input — not just the narration. It is their most-praised feature and it costs you nothing because you already have the data.
10. **Naming.** `code graph` / `code navigation` / `context filters` / `search contexts` are good, boring, enterprise-legible names. `search contexts` in particular (named, shareable, query-defined repo-sets) is a strong model for your `scope_key` — make scopes first-class, nameable, and shareable rather than opaque strings.
11. **Negative lesson worth internalising:** they removed embeddings from the code-context path. Put that in your design docs as external evidence for "embeddings never drive traversal."

## 5. Build-on vs. compete vs. consume

**Build on top of it? No.** Sourcegraph is closed source, starts at $16K/year minimum, and its only general-purpose fact store is flat string KVPs scoped to a repository with no provenance, no timestamp and no relationships. You physically cannot represent `(Service C) --reads_from--> (redis-notif-prod)` in it, let alone an assertion about that proposition. Building on it would mean: adopting a dependency you cannot read, cannot extend, cannot self-host free, and whose write API its own docs disclaim. The only thing you'd be renting is the code index — which you can rent *without* building on it (below).

**Compete? Only on the half they don't serve.** Do not build a code search engine, do not write SCIP indexers, do not try to beat `find_references`. Frame the product as *the layer above the code index*: the thing that remembers what was concluded, who concluded it, whether it's still true, and how the code connects to the running system. If a prospect already has Sourcegraph, that should make your pitch easier, not harder.

**Consume it as a data source? Yes — this is the highest-leverage conclusion in this document.**

Sourcegraph is an outstanding importer for fact source #2 (static analysis), delivered over an MCP server that is already in the agent's config. Concretely:

- **Call-site grain for free.** `find_references` + `go_to_definition` over MCP, or a SCIP index pulled directly, gives you compiler-accurate call edges at exactly the grain your design calls for — far better than LLM inference, and covering eight language ecosystems you will never implement.
- **Assert them with `method='code_derived'`, `asserted_by='sourcegraph'`, `scope_key='sourcegraph:<repo>@<commit>'`**, evidence `{repo, commit, path, line range}`, and run a scope sweep on each re-index. Their index replacement becomes your closed-world snapshot, with correct semantics, for free.
- **SCIP symbol strings become `entity_identifier` rows** with `authority='scip_symbol'` — strong ids that cannot over-merge.
- **`commit_search` / `diff_search` feed anchor integrity.** You need "has this file changed since the evidence commit"; `diff_search` answers it fleet-wide without cloning anything.
- **Deep Search becomes a bootstrap fact-miner.** Your risk #1 is fact density — agents restating what's in the one repo in front of them. Deep Search is explicitly cross-repo and returns cited sources. Running your three path templates as Deep Search questions and converting each cited answer into low-trust `llm_inferred` assertions with real evidence anchors is a cold-start strategy that costs you a subscription instead of six months. Your verification agent then promotes or refutes them.
- **The boundary is clean and defensible:** Sourcegraph knows repositories, files, commits and symbols. It does not know that a running service exists. Everything from `Redis cache E` rightwards — the half your motivating query actually turns on — is yours alone.

The uncomfortable corollary: if Sourcegraph is your best code-fact importer and cloud/OTel importers are your best infrastructure-fact importers, then **risk #1 is confirmed, not mitigated** — importers will out-produce agents, and the agent-asserted layer is annotation and correction on top of an imported spine. That is still a real product. But design the write path, the trust model and the sales pitch for *that* product, not for the one where agents produce most of the facts.

## Bottom line

Sourcegraph is the best system in the world for regenerating code-derived facts and the worst possible place to put one. It has no proposition/assertion split, no polarity, no bitemporality, no confidence, no cardinality, no alias/merge machinery, no `env`, no infrastructure, and no write path for a claim. Its temporality is git, its provenance is a tool name, its extensible store is a flat string tag on a repo, and its knowledge is recomputed on every question at up to five minutes and real LLM cost. Overlap with the Living AI Knowledge concept is genuine but narrow, concentrated entirely in call-site-grain cross-repo code facts and in Deep Search being a surprisingly strong substitute for hops 1–3 of the motivating query with zero setup.

Two things should worry the builder. First, Deep Search sets a high bar for *day-one usefulness* that a graph with an empty database cannot meet — you must have an answer for "why is this better than just asking Deep Search" on the first demo, and that answer has to be hops 4–5 plus accumulation. Second, they are already saying "continuously updated knowledge base" on their homepage.

Neither changes the strategic call: **do not compete with the index, consume it.**
